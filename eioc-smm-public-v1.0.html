Chapter 1

The Emotional Attack Surface

How social platforms amplify emotional manipulation—and why "smart people" are the most vulnerable.

Scammers do not attack your device. They attack your state.

AI-generated personas, deepfake endorsements, and high-tempo chat flows now bypass logic entirely. Social platforms amplify these attacks because urgency, fear, and excitement increase engagement. The result is a population that believes "I'd never fall for that"—even as fraud becomes the most common crime category in the UK.

The five emotional levers attackers use:

Urgency — "You must act now."
Authority pressure — "I'm calling from your bank."
Shame — "Don't tell anyone—they'll think you're foolish."
Isolation — "Your bank will block this; keep this between us."
Cognitive narrowing — "Just follow these steps quickly."

These levers work on everyone. They bypass logic and target the body's threat-response system.

The goal of this training is to help you recognize these levers as they are being pulled.

Chapter 2

Emotional Indicators of Compromise

Just as computers have Indicators of Compromise, humans have E‑IoCs—internal signals that something is wrong.

E‑IoC‑RUSH

Synthetic Urgency

Feeling rushed or pressured to act immediately. The tempo of the interaction feels imposed, not chosen.

Early cue: "Why do I feel like I can't pause?"
E‑IoC‑AUTH

False Authority Pressure

Feeling intimidated or deferential toward someone whose credentials you have not verified.

Early cue: "Why am I trusting this person's authority?"
E‑IoC‑NARROW

Cognitive Tunnel

Losing awareness of the bigger picture. Your attention collapses to the next instruction, then the next.

Early cue: "When did I stop thinking about the whole situation?"
E‑IoC‑SHAME

Induced Embarrassment

Feeling judged, embarrassed, or "stupid" for hesitating. This is the attacker's most powerful lever.

Early cue: "Why do I feel ashamed for being cautious?"
E‑IoC‑ISO

Isolation from Verification

Feeling like you shouldn't tell anyone. The other person is discouraging you from checking with someone you trust.

Early cue: "Why is someone asking me to keep this secret?"
E‑IoC‑OBLIG

Sunk-Cost Obligation

Feeling pressured to continue because you've "already started." The time or effort invested becomes a trap.

Early cue: "Am I continuing because I want to, or because I feel I have to?"
When you notice even one of these, you are already in the drift.

Chapter 3

The Drift Model

Emotional drift happens in four predictable stages. Understanding this sequence is the first step to interrupting it.

1

Hook

Curiosity, excitement, or fear draws you in. The content feels relevant, urgent, or too good to miss. A social media ad, a message from a "friend," a notification that demands attention.

2

Tempo Increase

The attacker speeds up the interaction. Questions are answered before you finish asking them. The pace feels collaborative but is controlled. You feel guided by someone competent.

3

Narrowing

Your attention collapses to the next step, then the next. The broader context—who is this person? Why am I doing this?—fades from awareness. Pausing feels like it would "break" the process.

4

Surrender

You hand over access, money, or information. By this stage, the emotional sequence has done its work. The transaction feels like a formality—not a decision.

The drift is not your fault. It is a designed psychological sequence. This training teaches you to interrupt it.

Grounding Prompts

These short questions break the trance and restore cognitive bandwidth:

"What changed in my body in the last 30 seconds?"
"Who benefits from me acting quickly?"
"What would I do if I had 10 minutes instead of 10 seconds?"
"Why is someone asking me to keep this secret?"
"Does this feel like my normal decision-making pace?"

Scenario 1

The Urgent Official

Social media ad → chat window Investment scam AI-generated endorsement
You see an ad featuring a trusted financial commentator endorsing an investment platform. The production quality is flawless—lighting, graphics, voice. You click through to a professional site and enter your phone number. A representative calls within minutes. They are calm, knowledgeable, unhurried. Over the next few minutes, they guide you through "verification steps" that include enabling screen-share. When you hesitate, they say: "Your bank will try to block this—they don't want you accessing opportunities like this."

Drift Sequence

Minute 1 — Curiosity → Mild Excitement
The landing page looks polished. You feel a small spark of opportunity. Nothing feels dangerous; it feels like discovering something insiders know.
Minute 3 — Authority Pressure → Urgency
The advisor uses your first name. The tempo increases. You feel a subtle shift: a sense of being guided by someone competent. The first tug of urgency appears.
Minute 5 — Cognitive Narrowing
Instructions come in a tight sequence: open settings, enable screen-share, confirm a code. Your attention narrows to the next step, then the next. The broader context fades.
Minute 7 — Shame Induction → Isolation
When you hesitate: "Don't tell your bank—they'll make it difficult." You feel a sudden flush of embarrassment, as if you're being naïve for hesitating. You are now isolated from verification.
This is the emotional point of compromise—not the transaction.
What is the strongest emotional indicator of compromise in this scenario?
The ad looked professional and believable
The advisor knew your name
Being told to keep the interaction secret from your bank
The investment returns seemed too good
At which stage did the drift become most dangerous?
When you clicked the ad
When the advisor called quickly
When instructions narrowed to step-by-step compliance
When returns were displayed

🛡️ E‑IoC Recognition Prompts

  • Why am I being asked to act before I can think?
  • Why is someone telling me not to consult my bank?
  • Does this pace feel like mine, or someone else's?

Scenario 2

The Helpful Fixer

Fake account recovery → chat window Remote access fraud Authority impersonation
You receive a message saying your account has been compromised. A "security specialist" offers to help you recover it immediately. They ask you to install a remote-access tool "so we can secure your account together." They are patient, knowledgeable, and reassuring. When you hesitate, they say: "We need to act fast before the hacker locks you out permanently. Don't tell anyone—we don't want to alert the attacker."

Drift Sequence

Minute 1 — Fear → Relief
The notification triggers a spike of fear. Then someone appears who can fix it. The relief is immediate and powerful—someone competent is helping you.
Minute 3 — Authority Pressure
"I'm from the security team." The language is calm and technical. You feel reassured. The fear shifts to trust.
Minute 5 — Cognitive Narrowing
"Just follow these steps quickly." Install this. Click here. Confirm that. Your attention narrows to compliance.
Minute 7 — Isolation
"Don't tell anyone—we need to act fast." You are now isolated from every person who could tell you this is wrong.
Which E‑IoC is the primary entry point in this scenario?
E‑IoC‑RUSH — synthetic urgency
E‑IoC‑SHAME — induced embarrassment
E‑IoC‑AUTH — false authority, combined with fear-to-relief dependency
E‑IoC‑OBLIG — sunk-cost obligation
What grounding prompt would be most effective at Minute 3?
Would a real company ask me to install remote-access software and keep it secret?
Is this the right number for tech support?
Should I call my friend first?
Is my password strong enough?

🛡️ E‑IoC Recognition Prompts

  • Would a real security team ask for remote access and secrecy simultaneously?
  • Is the relief I'm feeling being exploited to bypass my caution?
  • What would happen if I paused for 60 seconds?

Scenario 3

The Concerned Friend

AI voice clone or text Impersonation Emotional flooding
You receive a call or message from someone who sounds exactly like your son, daughter, or close friend. They are distressed: "I'm in trouble—please help. I need money now. Please don't call Mum." The voice is perfect. The panic is convincing. Every second you don't act feels like you're abandoning someone you love.

Drift Sequence

Minute 1 — Shock → Protective Instinct
The voice triggers an immediate emotional flood. Your body responds before your mind catches up. Every instinct says: protect this person.
Minute 3 — Urgency
"I need money now." The desperation is calibrated to prevent you from thinking. The tempo is set by the attacker's emotional performance.
Minute 5 — Narrowing
"Just send it—don't call anyone." Your attention collapses to the single action: transfer money. Everything else disappears.
Minute 7 — Shame
"Please don't tell Mum." The shame lever is deeply personal. You are being asked to protect a secret that benefits the attacker.
Why is this scenario uniquely dangerous?
The technology is new
It exploits the protective instinct—the deepest emotional lever available
The caller knows your name
It involves money
What is the most effective immediate response?
Send a smaller amount first to test
Hang up and call the person back on a number you already have
Ask them security questions
Ask to speak to someone else who is with them

🛡️ E‑IoC Recognition Prompts

  • Does this sound like them—or like a performance of them?
  • Why can't I verify this independently?
  • What would I do if I stepped outside for 30 seconds?

Scenario 4

The Secret Benefactor

Social media ad → DM Grant / loan scam Opportunity exploitation
A social media ad or DM tells you that you've been "selected" for a government grant, hardship loan, or special programme. The message is personalised—it references your location and situation. To claim the offer, you need to provide bank details and pay a small "processing fee." The tone is warm and congratulatory. You feel lucky.

Drift Sequence

Minute 1 — Excitement
A spark of hope. Someone has noticed you. Something good is happening. The emotional hook is not fear—it's optimism.
Minute 3 — Authority Pressure
The language mimics official communications. Logos, reference numbers, deadlines. You feel like this is legitimate because it looks legitimate.
Minute 5 — Narrowing
"Complete your application before the deadline." Your attention shifts to the form, the fields, the next step. The question "is this real?" fades.
Minute 7 — Obligation
"You're lucky—don't lose your spot." The sunk-cost lever activates. You've already invested hope and time. Walking away feels like losing something you almost had.
Which E‑IoC is most active in this scenario?
E‑IoC‑SHAME — feeling embarrassed
E‑IoC‑OBLIG — sunk-cost obligation combined with hope
E‑IoC‑ISO — feeling isolated
E‑IoC‑RUSH — synthetic urgency
What is the best grounding prompt for this type of attack?
Is the processing fee suspicious?
What would I do if I waited until tomorrow?
Should I ask my bank?
Is the logo correct?

🛡️ E‑IoC Recognition Prompts

  • Why is this urgent? Legitimate grants don't expire in minutes.
  • Why me? Did I apply for this?
  • Would a real government programme ask for fees via social media DM?
